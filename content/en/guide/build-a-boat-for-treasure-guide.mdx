## The Core Loop: Build, Sail, Earn Gold

The loop is straightforward on paper. You get a build plot, you construct a ship out of blocks, and you sail it through obstacle-filled stages. Progress earns gold, which feeds back into building. The official description does not enumerate stages, block names, gold amounts, or shop prices — so any article that quotes exact figures for those things is either citing a third-party guide or making them up.

That leaves players to learn by iteration. In practice, the useful mental model is that a boat is a set of competing pressures, and every design choice trades one against another. Community guides and player reasoning commonly frame those pressures this way:

- **Buoyancy:** enough volume and light enough mass to float rather than sink.
- **Weight distribution:** keeping the center of mass low and roughly centered so the craft does not tip or nose-dive.
- **Thrust and steering:** enough forward force to clear gaps and enough control to avoid hazards.
- **Protection:** shielding the parts that break first — often the front and the underside.

None of that is developer documentation. It is player reasoning about physical tradeoffs, and it holds up because the game's obstacles punish the same weaknesses repeatedly: top-heavy ships tip, fragile prows shatter, and underpowered boats stall.

## Design Reasoning That Survives Contact With Obstacles

The single most common beginner mistake, based on how players describe their early runs, is overbuilding. A huge, heavily armored craft sounds safe until you remember that mass has to be carried by whatever buoyancy you built. A ship that floats barely is a ship that floods the moment something knocks a block loose.

A calmer approach is to build small, test early, and add only what solves a problem you actually observed. If your boat flips on a turn, the fix is usually lower and wider, not more thrust. If it stalls climbing or crossing a gap, the fix is thrust or reduced mass. If the front keeps exploding, the fix is sacrificial structure at the tip rather than armoring the entire hull.

Build in layers rather than one solid mass. A distinct hull, deck, and propulsion section is easier to repair mentally — when a run fails, you can usually identify which layer failed. Keep a minimal test craft saved mentally as a baseline, so when you change one variable you can tell whether that change helped.
