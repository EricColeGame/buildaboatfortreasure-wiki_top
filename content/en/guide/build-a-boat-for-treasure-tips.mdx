## The Core Loop: Build, Sail, Earn

The structure is straightforward, and most of its appeal comes from how those three steps interact.

1. **Build.** On your plot, you place blocks and parts to construct a vessel. Structure, weight distribution, and propulsion all matter.
2. **Sail.** You launch your creation into a course filled with obstacles. Your boat either survives or it does not.
3. **Earn.** Reaching stages and treasure rewards you with gold, which feeds back into better builds.

Where most players spend their time is the middle step. A design that looks sturdy on the plot can flip, sink, or break apart the moment it hits the water or the first obstacle. That gap between planning and performance is the actual game.

### General Building Reasoning

The official sources do not publish block names, stats, or blueprint lists, so treat the following as **player reasoning** rather than documented fact:

- **Buoyancy and weight.** Anything you add increases mass. A wider footprint tends to be more stable than a tall, narrow tower, but it also presents more surface area to obstacles.
- **Thrust and control.** Forward power matters, but so does the ability to steer. A fast boat you cannot aim is usually worse than a slower one you can control.
- **Protection.** Placing your most important parts behind something disposable can save a run, even if it looks inelegant.
- **Iteration.** The fastest way to improve is to change one thing per attempt and watch what breaks first.

None of this is a guaranteed formula. It is the reasoning experienced players tend to apply, and your results will depend on the course and your own experimentation.

## A Sensible Way to Improve

Because the game does not hand you a stat sheet, improvement comes from observation. A practical routine looks like this:

- Run the same course several times with one design to establish a baseline.
- Note the first obstacle that consistently causes failure.
- Adjust only the part of the build that addresses that failure.
- Repeat until you clear it, then move to the next bottleneck.

This is slower than copying someone else's blueprint, but it teaches you why your boat behaves the way it does. That understanding transfers to every future design.

Also worth considering: the server size is seven. That means you are often sharing a course with a small group, which can make co-op play and shared experimentation easier than in a crowded server.
